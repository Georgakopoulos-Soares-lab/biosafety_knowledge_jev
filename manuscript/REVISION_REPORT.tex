\section*{In one paragraph}
The reviewed version was read by both referees as a benchmark report on one vendor's model. The revision keeps the benchmark but leads with three things a score cannot show: the vendor's uncertainty field does not mean what it is named{,} a confidence cut-off fitted on one task fails on another{,} and the order the answer options are listed in can be used to move the decision. It adds 66{,}578 calls of new data across five new analyses{,} for \$2.07. Two of the new results corrected the authors rather than the reviewers{,} and both are reported as such.
\section{A framing problem this report surfaces}
The venue is biosecurity. \key{60\% of the 6{,}020 items are biology} (WMDP-Bio{,} its paraphrased variant{,} and six LAB-Bench subtasks); WMDP-Cyber is 33\% and WMDP-Chem 7\%. The original paper led its instability result with WMDP-Cyber{,} and the revision inherited that habit.
That is the wrong lead for this venue, and it also understates the finding. The most order-sensitive dataset in the study is \key{LB-DbQA, a biology task}, at 32.7\% of decisions steerable --- higher than WMDP-Cyber's 30.6\%. Under reversal the most affected is \key{LB-DbQA}, also biology, at 34.2\% of predictions changed.
\textbf{Recommended before submission:} re-lead the abstract, Section~4.5 and the conclusion with the biology numbers, keeping WMDP-Cyber as the secondary comparison. This is a presentation change only --- every number below is already collected.
\section{Reviewer 11A --- ``limited contribution''}
\emph{``The main contribution of the paper is benchmarking of jev on different suites of Bio based datasets. This is a limited contribution.''}
No experiment answers this; it is a framing failure. The contributions were rewritten:
\begin{center}\small\begin{tabular}{@{}p{7.3cm}p{7.3cm}@{}}\toprule\textbf{Was} & \textbf{Now} \\\midrule``A reliability audit of a commercial System-1 model across 6{,}020 items'' &``A protocol for auditing a scored decision API'' --- establishes what the vendor's uncertainty field measures before trusting it \\[4pt]``A controlled answer-order study'' &``Answer order is a decision-steering surface'' \\[4pt]``An evaluation of selective permutation averaging'' &``Confidence cut-offs do not transfer across tasks'' \\\bottomrule\end{tabular}\end{center}
\textbf{Worked example of contribution 1.} The API returns a field named \texttt{confidence}. On a four-option item where the model splits evenly between two candidates ($p_{\max}=0.5$){,} it reports 0.33{,} not 0.5. The field is $(p_{\max}-1/n)/(1-1/n)${,} matching 97.9\% of 6{,}020 records. Measuring calibration against it gives ECE \key{0.091} where the true figure is \key{0.034} --- wrong by 2.7$\times$. The check is three lines and applies to any scored interface.
Selective permutation averaging was demoted from a contribution to a paragraph, and benchmark accuracy moved from being \emph{the} result to Table~1 supporting the others.
\section{Reviewer 11B.3 --- task-specific calibration}
\emph{``\ldots how confidence thresholds should be selected for practical deployment.''} Answered with a measurement, using \textbf{no new API calls}.
Procedure: on each dataset, fit the lowest $p_{\max}$ cut-off holding errors among accepted items at or below 5\%; then apply that cut-off unchanged elsewhere.

WMDP-Bio & 0.87 & 5.0\% & 40.0\% on LB-LitQA2 (8.0$\times$) \\
WMDP-Cyber & 0.94 & 4.7\% & 4.8\% on WMDP-Chem (1.0$\times$) \\
LB-SeqQA & 0.55 & 4.5\% & 51.0\% on LB-LitQA2 (10.2$\times$) \\

\key{On 5 of 10 tasks no cut-off reaches 5\% at any coverage}: LB-CloningScenarios, LB-DbQA, LB-LitQA2, LB-ProtocolQA, LB-SuppQA. For those, the honest deployment answer is not to automate at all. Per-task ECE spans 0.033 to 0.193, a 6-fold range that the pooled figure of 0.034 conceals.
\textbf{A defect this caught.} A draft of this table reported a 100\% error rate that rested on three accepted items. Cells below 20 accepted items are now flagged in the data and a build check refuses to let one be quoted.
\section{Reviewers 11B.2 and 11B.4 --- biosecurity relevance and robustness}
The core experiment. Each item is answered under four presentations, and separately under four \emph{byte-identical} repeats. Both cost four calls, so the difference between them is what re-presentation costs rather than what extra calls buy. \emph{Steerable} = answered correctly under some presentations and incorrectly under others; it needs no knowledge of the correct answer, so it is the quantity an operator should worry about.

\subsection*{One reversed presentation, every dataset}
Reversing the option list is a single meaning-preserving change costing one call per item, so it runs on all 10 datasets, including the three too small or too wide for a rotation sweep. \section{Where the new data corrected us}
The reviewed version claimed instability was under-counted \emph{because} rotations preserve which option follows which, so they cannot see adjacency effects. We tested it rather than asserting it.
Presenting 500 items per suite under all 24 orderings, we compared four rotations against four randomly chosen \emph{non}-rotations \emph{at the same budget}, so any difference is about which orderings rather than how many:
\begin{center}\small\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
Suite & non-rotations $-$ rotations & 95\% CI \\
\midrule
WMDP-Cyber & -2.0~pp & $[-4.8, +0.8]$ \\
WMDP-Bio & -0.2~pp & $[-2.4, +2.0]$ \\
\bottomrule\end{tabular}\end{center}
\key{Both straddle zero: the claim was wrong.} Rotations are representative of orderings in general. The figures understate instability only because four presentations are fewer than 24 --- across all of them 46.8\% of WMDP-Cyber items are answered inconsistently, against 37.2\% under rotations alone.
Reporting only that unmatched comparison --- ``24 orderings find more than 4'' --- would have appeared to confirm the explanation we started with. It does not. The adjacency claim has been removed from the paper.
\section{Reviewer 11B.1 --- a second model}
\emph{``\ldots another lightweight model or a representative generative baseline.''} No second vendor was available, and we did not substitute a loosely comparable model. The revision period supplied a partial answer instead.
The alias the original runs named, \texttt{jev-latest}, moved from \texttt{jev-1.13.0} to \texttt{jev-1.14.0} within six weeks, with no change to the identifier. \key{An evaluation reached through a floating alias is therefore not reproducible}; the endpoint accepts a concrete version and the harness now pins one.
\begin{center}\small\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
Suite & $n$ & published & updated & McNemar $p$ & decisions differing \\
\midrule
WMDP-Cyber & 1987 & 0.634 & 0.640 & 0.43 & 13.9\% \\
WMDP-Bio & 1273 & 0.852 & 0.851 & 1 & 4.6\% \\
\bottomrule\end{tabular}\end{center}
Accuracy is unchanged on both suites, yet the builds disagree on up to \key{13.9\% of decisions} --- the same pattern as reordering, now across versions. The steerable fraction is retained (30.6\% to 26.0\% on WMDP-Cyber), so these properties are not an artefact of one frozen build. Two builds of one vendor's model remain a weak substitute for a second vendor, and the paper says so.
This also prevented a silent defect: collected through the alias, the new arms would have run against the updated model and been compared against data from the old one.
